Problem 4 · IMU-based indoor positioning for drones and robots

Strapdown Field Guide

An engineering guide for a five-person team: calibrate a real MPU6050, build an error-state Kalman filter around it, aid it with motion constraints that drones and robots actually provide, run it on an ESP32, and prove every claim with a measurement.

Start with the runbook: it sequences the whole project, milestone by milestone, with the commands of the starter repository imu-nav. The code reference says what every file does, what to configure and how to modify it. Chapters 00 to 09 are the shared foundation; each member then follows their lane in Chapter 09. Chapters 10 to 12 are the estimator, 13 to 15 aiding and data, 16 to 19 implementation, evaluation, demo and reference sheets.

Chapter 02

The estimation problem

The task is not "read an IMU and integrate". It is to estimate a state that the IMU only observes indirectly, through a model, with errors that grow without bound unless something outside the IMU constrains them.

Frames and notation

One convention is used through the whole guide and the code. Mixing conventions is the most common way a mathematically correct filter produces nonsense.

SymbolMeaning
\(\{w\}\)World frame. Origin at the start marker, \(x\) along the track, \(z\) up (ENU-like, local).
\(\{b\}\)Body frame of the sensor box. \(x\) forward, \(y\) left, \(z\) up.
\(\{s\}\)Raw sensor axes of the MPU6050. Related to \(\{b\}\) by the mounting rotation and by calibration.
\(\mathbf{R} \equiv \mathbf{R}_{wb}\)Rotation that maps a vector from body to world: \(\mathbf{v}^w = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{v}^b\).
\(\mathbf{q}\)Hamilton unit quaternion for the same rotation, scalar first: \(\mathbf{q} = [q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]^\top\).
\(\mathbf{g}^w = [0, 0, -g]^\top\)Gravity in the world frame, with \(g\) the local value (about \(9.806\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) near Lausanne; compute it, do not assume 9.81).
\(\mathbf{f}^b\)Specific force, what the accelerometer measures.
\(\boldsymbol{\omega}^b\)Angular rate of \(\{b\}\) relative to \(\{w\}\), expressed in \(\{b\}\).

The accelerometer does not measure acceleration. It measures the non-gravitational force per unit mass:

\[ \mathbf{f}^b = \mathbf{R}^\top\left(\mathbf{a}^w - \mathbf{g}^w\right) \]

At rest, \(\mathbf{a}^w = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\mathbf{f}^b = \mathbf{R}^\top[0,0,g]^\top\): a level box reads \(+g\) on its up axis. If your logged data reads \(-g\) at rest on the up axis, your axis convention or sign is wrong. This is test A3 in Chapter 06.

State, and what the IMU alone can and cannot observe

The navigation state is position \(\mathbf{p}^w\), velocity \(\mathbf{v}^w\), attitude \(\mathbf{q}\), and the two sensor biases \(\mathbf{b}_a, \mathbf{b}_g\). Integrating the IMU propagates it:

\[ \dot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{v}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{f}_m - \mathbf{b}_a\right) + \mathbf{g}^w, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0\\ \boldsymbol{\omega}_m - \mathbf{b}_g\end{bmatrix} \]
StateObservable from the IMU alone?Why
Roll, pitchYes, when not acceleratingGravity is a known reference direction.
Yaw (heading)NoRotation about gravity leaves the gravity measurement unchanged. Needs a magnetometer, a map or a known start alignment.
VelocityNoOnly its derivative is measured. Needs ZUPT, odometry or flow.
PositionNoOnly its second derivative is measured. Needs a position-type aid (map, tags).
Gyro biasPartly, when stationaryAt rest the true rate is zero, so the reading is the bias (ZARU).
Accel biasPartlyHorizontal accel bias and tilt error produce the same signal at rest and cannot be separated without rotation.

Between stops, if the box tilts, the two parts behave differently and the estimate is partly wrong. Turning the box (as at the 25 m turnaround) is what separates them.

How fast pure inertial errors grow

Three error sources dominate. Each derivation below assumes a level platform, small angles, constant bias over the interval and no aiding. Schuler oscillation (period about 84 minutes) is irrelevant on the time scales here.

Derivation Accelerometer bias: \(\delta p_a = \tfrac12 b_a t^2\)
  1. A constant residual bias \(b_a\) [m/s²] along a horizontal axis is integrated as if it were real acceleration: \(\delta\dot v = b_a\).
  2. Integrate once from \(\delta v(0) = 0\): \(\delta v(t) = b_a t\) [m/s].
  3. Integrate again from \(\delta p(0) = 0\): \[ \delta p_a(t) = \int_0^t b_a \tau\, d\tau = \tfrac12 b_a t^2 \ \ [\mathrm{m}] \]
  4. Numbers: \(b_a = 1\ \mathrm{mg} = 9.81\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), \(t = 30\) s gives \(\delta p_a = 0.5 \times 9.81\times10^{-3}\times 900 = 4.4\) m. Tier 1 asks for under 1 m at 30 s, so a 1 mg residual alone already fails without aiding.
Derivation Tilt error: \(\delta p_\theta = \tfrac12 g\,\delta\theta\, t^2\)
  1. An attitude error \(\delta\theta\) [rad] in roll or pitch rotates the estimated gravity vector. Subtracting the wrong gravity leaves a horizontal residual \(g\sin\delta\theta \approx g\,\delta\theta\).
  2. This acts exactly like an accelerometer bias of size \(g\,\delta\theta\). Applying the previous result: \[ \delta p_\theta(t) = \tfrac12\, g\,\delta\theta\, t^2 \]
  3. Numbers: \(\delta\theta = 0.1^\circ = 1.745\times10^{-3}\) rad gives an equivalent bias of \(0.0171\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) (1.7 mg). At 30 s, \(\delta p_\theta = 7.7\) m.
  4. Consequence: a tenth of a degree of tilt error costs more than a 1 mg bias. Attitude quality is a position-accuracy problem.
Derivation Gyro bias: \(\delta p_g = \tfrac16 g\, b_g\, t^3\)
  1. A residual roll or pitch rate bias \(b_g\) [rad/s] makes the tilt error grow linearly: \(\delta\theta(t) = b_g t\).
  2. The horizontal acceleration error is then \(g\,\delta\theta(t) = g\, b_g t\).
  3. Integrate twice: \(\delta v(t) = \tfrac12 g\, b_g t^2\) and \[ \delta p_g(t) = \tfrac16 g\, b_g\, t^3 \]
  4. Numbers: \(b_g = 0.01^\circ/\mathrm{s} = 1.745\times10^{-4}\) rad/s, \(t = 30\) s: \(\delta p_g = \tfrac16 \times 9.81 \times 1.745\times10^{-4}\times 27000 = 7.7\) m.
  5. Limitation: in practice the accelerometer corrects roll and pitch through Mahony or the ESKF, so tilt does not grow freely. The cubic law describes the unaided worst case. A yaw-rate bias does not couple into gravity; it rotates the direction of travel instead (Chapter 13, NHC and line constraint).
Derivation White noise: random-walk growth

Bias errors are deterministic for a given run: they push in one direction. White noise produces a spread across runs. With accelerometer noise density \(N_a\) [m/s²/√Hz] and gyro noise density \(N_g\) [rad/s/√Hz]:

  1. Velocity: integrating white noise gives a Wiener process, \(\sigma_v(t) = N_a\sqrt{t}\). This is velocity random walk (VRW).
  2. Position: integrating a Wiener process \(W\) gives variance \(\mathrm{Var}\left[\int_0^t W(s)\,ds\right] = t^3/3\), so \(\sigma_p(t) = N_a\, t^{3/2}/\sqrt3\).
  3. Angle: \(\sigma_\theta(t) = N_g\sqrt t\) (angle random walk, ARW). Through gravity it adds horizontal acceleration \(g\,\theta(t)\); integrating a Wiener process twice gives variance \(t^5/20\), so \(\sigma_{p,\theta}(t) = g\,N_g\, t^{5/2}/\sqrt{20}\).
  4. MPU6050 datasheet values: accel noise density \(400\ \mu g/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\) and gyro rate noise density \(0.005^\circ/\mathrm{s}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\) fact. Converted: \(N_a = 3.92\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(N_g = 8.73\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/s/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Measure your own boards; the datasheet is typical, not guaranteed.

Chapter 03

Drift explorer

Two models on one plot. The dashed curves are the closed-form free-inertial errors from Chapter 02. The solid curve is a Monte Carlo of a simplified stop-and-go model of our trolley run. Neither is a measurement. Physical results replace the defaults after milestone M2.

Model Assumptions of the stop-and-go Monte Carlo
  1. The trolley alternates between moving a distance \(s\) at speed \(v\) (duration \(T_m = s/v\)) and stopping for \(T_s\). The 25 m out-and-back gives \(50/s\) moving segments.
  2. At each stop, ZUPT resets velocity error to zero and re-estimates the combined horizontal accel error. Averaging white noise over \(T_s\) leaves a residual with standard deviation \(N_a/\sqrt{T_s}\). Unmodelled effects (temperature drift, tilt changes while moving, scale errors) are lumped into the floor \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}\). The per-segment accel error is drawn from \(\mathcal N\!\left(0,\ N_a^2/T_s + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}^2\right)\).
  3. Along-track error added in a segment: \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_a T_m^2\) plus noise with \(\sigma = N_a T_m^{3/2}/\sqrt3\). Segments are independent because the bias is re-estimated at each stop.
  4. ZARU at each stop re-estimates the yaw-rate bias to \(\sigma = N_g/\sqrt{T_s}\), plus a floor of one tenth of the gyro residual. Heading error accumulates across segments and is never reset by ZUPT or ZARU: \(\delta\psi_k = \delta\psi_{k-1} + \varepsilon_{g,k} T_m + \mathcal N(0, N_g^2 T_m)\).
  5. Cross-track error grows by \(s\,\delta\psi\) per segment. With the straight-line map constraint enabled, cross-track error is removed entirely; only along-track error remains.
  6. Not modelled: errors during the turnaround, scale-factor error on turning, timestamp errors, outliers, filter tuning errors. Real errors will be larger. The model exists to show which levers matter (stop spacing, stop duration, residual floor), not to predict a score.
What the explorer teaches Free-inertial error crosses 1 m within seconds for any realistic MEMS residual. With stops, error is driven by \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_a T_m^2\), so it scales with the square of the time between stops. Halving the stop spacing quarters the per-segment error. Stop spacing is the cheapest accuracy lever you have, and it is a legitimate robot behaviour: waypoint stops.

Key technology decisions

Decision Where does the ESKF run?

Requirement. A real-time estimate on an embedded target (15 points), at IMU rate (200 Hz, so a 5 ms budget per step).

Alternatives. (a) Full ESKF on the ESP32. (b) ESP32 acquires and runs Mahony; host runs the ESKF. (c) Host only.

Trade-offs. (c) does not satisfy "embedded". (b) satisfies it weakly. (a) is strongest, and the original ESP32 has a single-precision FPU and 520 KB SRAM, so a dense 15×15 float32 covariance (900 bytes) and its temporaries are trivially small fact. A dense prediction \(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^\top\) costs about \(2n^3 = 6750\) multiply-adds for \(n = 15\) derived.

Selected. (a), with the Python float64 implementation run on the same logs as the reference.

Verification. M5 benchmark: measured cycles per predict and per update with esp_cpu_get_cycle_count(); position difference between ESP32 and Python on identical input.

Change if. The measured predict time exceeds roughly 2.5 ms (half the budget). Then exploit the sparsity of \(\mathbf{F}\), propagate covariance every second sample, or fall back to (b). Execution time is not assumed; it is measured.

Chapter 05

Track and ground truth

You build the track, so build it to produce clean evidence.

Build specification

  1. Lay a 25 m straight line with tape, measured with a long tape measure, not by pacing. Record the measured marker positions to the centimetre; these numbers are the ground truth.
  2. Mark every 5 m with a cross line. Five metres keeps the time between stops near 10 s at 0.5 m/s, which the drift explorer shows is the main accuracy lever. You may add 2.5 m sub-marks for a second stop-spacing experiment.
  3. Make a start fixture: a block taped at the 0 m line that the sensor box (on the trolley) butts against, so the initial position and heading are repeatable. Initial yaw is defined by this fixture, and the report says so.
  4. Keep the box on the trolley, with a rigid mount and a pointer at its reference point, so the pointer crosses each marker at a known spot.

Ground truth by timestamped markers

A push button wired to an ESP32 GPIO is pressed when the pointer is over a marker during the stop. The firmware records the IMU timestamp of the press. Ground truth is then a list of \((t_k, s_k)\) pairs, where \(s_k\) is the surveyed position along the line. At each press the true position is \(\mathbf{p}^w_k = [s_k, 0, 0]^\top\).

Independence rule The marker times and positions are used only for evaluation, never inside the filter. The map constraint uses only the shape of the path: "the box stays on the line \(y = 0\)". Feeding marker positions into the filter would turn the evaluation into a measurement of itself.

Defining "position error"

The organizers have not specified a metric open. Report all of these from every run, so any definition they use can be read off:

MetricDefinition
Checkpoint error\(e_k = \left\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}(t_k) - \mathbf{p}^w_k\right\|\) at each marker press.
Maximum and RMS errorOver all checkpoints of the run.
Error relative to distance\(e_k / d_k\), with \(d_k\) the true distance travelled up to marker \(k\) (counting the return leg).
Return-to-start error\(e\) at the final stop at 0 m, plus its along-track and cross-track components.
First-30-seconds errorMaximum \(e_k\) among presses within 30 s of motion start (Tier 1).

No post-hoc alignment on test runs. The world frame is fixed by the start fixture before the run. Rotating or shifting the estimated trajectory afterwards to fit the markers would artificially improve the score. If an alignment is ever computed, it is computed on a separate calibration run and then frozen.

Run protocol

  1. Power on, wait for the temperature to settle (logged), then a 10 s static period at the start fixture for initial alignment and bias estimates.
  2. Push at a steady pace to each marker, stop for the configured stop duration (3 s default), press the button, continue.
  3. Turn the trolley 180° at the 25 m marker while stationary over it, then continue back.
  4. End at the start fixture, hold 10 s, stop logging.
  5. Repeat at least five times per configuration. Report the mean and spread across runs, not the best run.

Timestamps

The timestamp that matters is when the sample was measured, not when the code read it. Two-step method:

  1. The data-ready ISR stores esp_timer_get_time() (µs) and increments a sample counter.
  2. Each sample read from the FIFO gets a sequence number. Its timestamp is reconstructed from the latest ISR time and the number of samples still in the FIFO, times the nominal period.
  3. Log both the reconstructed timestamp and the sequence number. Gaps in the sequence reveal dropped samples; the timestamps reveal jitter.

Why it matters: every propagation step multiplies by \(\Delta t\). A timing error \(\delta(\Delta t)\) produces a velocity error \(a\,\delta(\Delta t)\) per step, and an error in the assumed sample rate acts as a scale error on every integrated quantity.

A 180° turn with a 1 % gyro scale error leaves 1.8° of heading error. On the return leg that becomes cross-track drift of about \(0.031 \times 25 = 0.8\) m at the start point without a map constraint derived. Gyro scale calibration is therefore directly visible in your return-to-start error.

Basic calibration, for comparison

Six-position accelerometer calibration places each axis up and down. Per axis, bias and scale follow from the two readings: \(b = -\tfrac12(r_+ + r_-)\), \(s = 2g/(r_+ - r_-)\). It cannot identify misalignment, depends on the faces being truly vertical, and uses only six data points. Static gyro bias is the mean of a still recording. Both are useful as a first sanity check and as the "simple" baseline in the report, against which the full method must show held-out improvement.

The multi-position method (Tedaldi, Pretto, Menegatti, ICRA 2014)

The method used by IMU-TK. You hold the sensor still in 36 to 50 orientations by hand, with smooth motion between them. Static intervals are detected automatically. The accelerometer is calibrated from the static poses alone; the gyroscope is then calibrated from the rotations between them, using the calibrated accelerometer as the reference fact.

Model Calibration frame and parameter count

The calibrated, orthogonal frame \(\{O\}\) is defined from the accelerometer: its \(x\) axis coincides with the accelerometer \(x\) axis and its \(y\) axis lies in the plane of the accelerometer \(x\) and \(y\) axes. This removes three rotational degrees of freedom, so the accelerometer needs only three non-orthogonality angles.

\[ \mathbf{a}^O = \mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a\left(\mathbf{a}^S + \mathbf{b}_a\right),\quad \mathbf{T}_a = \begin{bmatrix}1 & -\alpha_{yz} & \alpha_{zy}\\ 0 & 1 & -\alpha_{zx}\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{K}_a = \mathrm{diag}(s^a_x, s^a_y, s^a_z) \]

Nine accelerometer parameters: three angles, three scales, three biases. The gyro axes must be mapped into the same frame, so it needs six misalignment terms:

\[ \boldsymbol{\omega}^O = \mathbf{T}_g\mathbf{K}_g\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}^S + \mathbf{b}_g\right),\quad \mathbf{T}_g = \begin{bmatrix}1 & -\gamma_{yz} & \gamma_{zy}\\ \gamma_{xz} & 1 & -\gamma_{zx}\\ -\gamma_{xy} & \gamma_{yx} & 1\end{bmatrix} \]

Nine gyro parameters are optimised (six angles, three scales). The gyro bias is fixed beforehand as minus the mean over the initial static period: \(\mathbf{b}_g = -\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}^S}\big|_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\).

Sign convention: the paper adds the bias to the raw reading. The filter chapter subtracts an estimated bias from a measurement. Both are fine; the code must use one, stated once, in calib.yaml.

Derivation Static detector and the parameterless threshold
  1. Over a sliding window of length \(t_w\) (about 1 to 2 s), compute the variance of each accelerometer axis and their magnitude: \[ \varsigma(t) = \sqrt{\mathrm{var}_{t_w}(a_x)^2 + \mathrm{var}_{t_w}(a_y)^2 + \mathrm{var}_{t_w}(a_z)^2} \]
  2. Compute \(\varsigma_{\mathrm{init}}\) over the initial static period, which is known to be still.
  3. Classify a sample as static when \(\varsigma(t)^2 < k\,\varsigma_{\mathrm{init}}^2\).
  4. Instead of choosing \(k\) by hand, run the full accelerometer fit for \(k = 1, 2, \dots, 10\) and keep the \(k\) with the smallest residual. The data select the threshold; this is why the method is called parameterless.
  5. Average each detected interval to one vector \(\bar{\mathbf{a}}^S_j\). Discard intervals shorter than a minimum (for example 1 s), because short intervals are often transitions.
Derivation Accelerometer objective
  1. In a static pose the only specific force is the reaction to gravity, so the calibrated reading must have norm \(g\) whatever the orientation.
  2. Residual for pose \(j\): \(r_j(\boldsymbol\theta_a) = g^2 - \left\|\mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a(\bar{\mathbf{a}}^S_j + \mathbf{b}_a)\right\|^2\).
  3. Objective: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_a = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta_a}\sum_{j=1}^{M} r_j(\boldsymbol\theta_a)^2 \]
  4. Solve with Levenberg-Marquardt from \(\mathbf{T}_a = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{K}_a = \mathbf{I}\) (scaled to counts if fitting raw counts), \(\mathbf{b}_a = \mathbf{0}\).
  5. Observability: one scalar equation per pose, nine unknowns, so at least nine poses, well spread over the sphere of directions. With only face-up and face-down poses, the angles are poorly determined. Check conditioning from the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}\) at the solution: parameter covariance \(\approx \hat\sigma^2(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}\). Report the resulting standard deviations.
  6. Local gravity: an error \(\delta g/g\) appears directly as a common scale error on all three axes. Use a gravity formula with latitude and altitude for the venue.
Derivation Gyroscope objective, and why static poses are not enough
  1. Static poses identify only the gyro bias (the reading when the true rate is zero). Scale and misalignment multiply the true rate, which is zero at rest, so they are invisible in static data. They are identified only from the rotations between poses derived.
  2. For each pair of consecutive static poses \(j-1, j\), take the calibrated gravity directions \(\mathbf{u}_{j} = \mathbf{a}^O_j / \|\mathbf{a}^O_j\|\).
  3. Integrate the calibrated gyro over the motion between them, using RK4 on the quaternion with normalisation after each step: \[ \dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega)\,\mathbf{q},\qquad \boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega) = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -\boldsymbol\omega^\top\\ \boldsymbol\omega & -[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf{q}_{k+1} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4)}{\left\|\mathbf{q}_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4)\right\|} \] with \(\mathbf{k}_1 = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega_k)\mathbf{q}_k\), \(\mathbf{k}_2 = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega_{k+\frac12})(\mathbf{q}_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_1)\), \(\mathbf{k}_3 = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega_{k+\frac12})(\mathbf{q}_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_2)\), \(\mathbf{k}_4 = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega_{k+1})(\mathbf{q}_k + \Delta t\,\mathbf{k}_3)\), and \(\boldsymbol\omega_{k+\frac12}\) interpolated.
  4. This gives the rotation \(\mathbf{R}_j(\boldsymbol\theta_g)\) of the body from pose \(j-1\) to pose \(j\). A fixed world vector seen in the new body frame is rotated by the inverse, so the predicted gravity direction is \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_j = \mathbf{R}_j^\top\mathbf{u}_{j-1}\).
  5. Objective: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_g = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta_g}\sum_{j=2}^{M}\left\|\mathbf{u}_j - \mathbf{R}_j(\boldsymbol\theta_g)^\top\mathbf{u}_{j-1}\right\|^2 \]
  6. Unobservable direction: a rotation purely about gravity leaves \(\mathbf{u}\) unchanged and contributes nothing. The motions between poses must tilt the box about varied horizontal axes, and over the session every gyro axis must be rotated substantially.
  7. Dependency: the gyro fit uses the calibrated accelerometer as its reference, so accelerometer errors propagate into gyro parameters. Always fit the accelerometer first and validate it before the gyro.

Data collection protocol

  1. Tape the MPU6050 rigidly inside a box with flat faces (a 3D-printed cube or a small wooden block). The same mount is used on the trolley afterwards, so calibration and use share one sensor-to-box geometry.
  2. Power on and log for 10 to 15 minutes to warm up. Record temperature.
  3. Place the box still on a stable, non-vibrating table for 50 s (initial static period).
  4. Move it smoothly by hand to a new orientation and hold still for 2 to 4 s. Use the six faces, then leaning poses against a wall or wedge at many intermediate angles. Aim for 40 or more poses. Rotate about every axis between poses.
  5. Total session length a few minutes, so bias can be treated as constant.
  6. Repeat the full session three times, on both MPU6050 boards.

Chapter 08

Allan deviation

The tool that turns a static recording into the noise parameters of the filter. It also tells you whether a bias can be treated as constant over a stop, a run, or a whole session.

Derivation Definition and computation
  1. From a static rate signal \(\Omega(t)\) sampled at \(\Delta t\), form the integrated angle \(\theta_k = \sum_{i \le k}\Omega_i\Delta t\).
  2. For cluster time \(\tau = m\Delta t\), the overlapping Allan variance is \[ \sigma^2(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2(N - 2m)}\sum_{k=1}^{N-2m}\left(\theta_{k+2m} - 2\theta_{k+m} + \theta_k\right)^2 \]
  3. Equivalently, \(\sigma^2(\tau)\) is half the mean squared difference between consecutive averages of length \(\tau\).
  4. Compute for logarithmically spaced \(m\) and plot \(\sigma(\tau)\) on log-log axes, per axis.
Derivation Reading the slopes
NoiseSlopeRead offUnits (gyro)

White noise of density \(N\) gives \(\sigma(\tau) = N/\sqrt\tau\), hence the \(-\tfrac12\) slope and the reading at \(\tau = 1\) s. Rate random walk gives \(\sigma(\tau) = K\sqrt{\tau/3}\), hence the reading at \(\tau = 3\) s.

Derivation From Allan parameters to the filter's \(\mathbf{Q}\)
  1. White measurement noise of density \(N\) has continuous-time spectral density \(N^2\). Sampled at \(\Delta t\), its per-sample variance is \(\sigma_d^2 = N^2/\Delta t\).
  2. In the ESKF, accelerometer noise enters velocity error and gyro noise enters attitude error integrated over one step, so the discrete process noise blocks are \(\mathbf{Q}_v = N_a^2\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}_3\) and \(\mathbf{Q}_\theta = N_g^2\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}_3\) (to first order).
  3. Bias as a random walk with coefficient \(K\): \(\mathbf{Q}_b = K^2\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}_3\).
  4. Bias instability is not a random walk. A common, defensible approximation is a random walk tuned so its growth matches the flat region over the time scale of a run. State that approximation in the report.
  5. Then inflate deliberately for effects the static test cannot see (vibration while moving, temperature change, residual scale error), and justify the inflation by the innovation statistics (Chapters 11 and 17). Numbers taken straight from a quiet table are usually overconfident.

Orchestrator rules

The firmware builds for the original ESP32 with ESP-IDF v5.3.1. None of it has run on your ESP32 and MPU6050 yet. Gates M1 and M5 of the runbook exist to close that gap. Treat every hardware-facing default (pins, signs, timing) as assumed until its acceptance test passes.

Dataset folder format

One folder per recording. All files are CSV with a header line; time is integer microseconds on the IMU clock; units are SI. Writers: nav.io.save, tools/logger.py, the converters. Reader: nav.io.load(folder), which returns a Dataset with .imu, .markers, .gt, .mag, .aux_vel, .aux_pos, .config and the shortcut .aux (the three optional streams as a dict for runner.run).

Modification recipes

Each recipe lists the files, the code, and the test that proves the change. Do them on a branch, on synthetic data first.

R1 Change the sensor ranges (for example ±2 g, ±250 °/s)

Smaller ranges give finer resolution (16384 LSB/g, 131 LSB/(°/s)) but saturate sooner. On a pushed trolley ±2 g and ±250 °/s are enough; handheld turns can exceed 250 °/s, so keep ±500 there. Four places must change together:

# firmware/main/mpu6050.c
wr(R_GYRO_CONFIG, 0x00);     /* FS_SEL 0: +-250 dps  (0x08 = 500, 0x10 = 1000, 0x18 = 2000) */
wr(R_ACCEL_CONFIG, 0x00);    /* AFS_SEL 0: +-2 g     (0x08 = 4,   0x10 = 8,    0x18 = 16)   */
# firmware/main/mpu6050.h
#define MPU_ACC_LSB_PER_G 16384.0f
#define MPU_GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS 131.0f
# tools/protocol.py
ACC_LSB_PER_G, GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS, G0 = 16384.0, 131.0, 9.80665
# nav/synth.py  (ErrorModel defaults)
lsb_acc: float = G0 / 16384
lsb_gyro: float = np.radians(1 / 131.0)

Then: rebuild and flash, rerun the A3 faces test, and recalibrate: scale factors and biases change with range. Old calib.yaml files are invalid.

R2 Sensor mounted rotated or upside down

Describe the body axes in sensor axes and pass it at calibration. The string must be a proper rotation (determinant +1); the code refuses a reflection.

# chip upside down, x still forward:      body x = sensor x, body y = -sensor y, body z = -sensor z
python scripts/run_calib.py runs/calib_1 --holdout runs/calib_2 --lat 12.97 --alt 920 --mount "x,-y,-z"
# chip rotated 90 deg about z (sensor y points forward):
python scripts/run_calib.py ... --mount "y,-x,z"
python tools/gen_calib_header.py calib.yaml --noise noise.yaml     # folds R_bs into Ta and Tg

Calibration itself runs in the sensor frame; CalibParams.apply_acc computes \(\mathbf{a}_B = \mathbf{R}_{BS}\,\mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a(\mathbf{a}_S + \mathbf{b}_a)\). Check: flat on the floor, body z reads +g; pushed forward, body x acceleration is positive (test_mount_matrix covers the arithmetic, not your box).

R3 A different straight track (length, spacing) or a non-straight one

Straight, other spacing: only data changes. Tape the markers, then

python tools/logger.py /dev/ttyUSB0 runs/251031_1400_track_1 --positions 0,4,8,12,16,20,24,20,16,12,8,4,0
python scripts/make_synth.py track data/synth_t4 --spacing 4          # matching rehearsal

For a 50 m one-way line instead of 25 m out and back, use --positions 0,5,...,50; return-to-start is then not measured, so keep out-and-back if you want that metric.

L-shape or loop: markers need two coordinates. Store them in config.yaml and change the truth in evaluate.py; disable the line constraint (use_line) or apply it only on a segment:

# config.yaml of the run
marker_xy: [[0,0],[5,0],[10,0],[10,5],[10,10]]
# nav/evaluate.py, checkpoint_errors(est, markers, xy=None)
if xy is not None:
    truth = np.column_stack([np.asarray(xy)[markers[keep, 1].astype(int)], np.zeros(len(tk))])
else:
    truth = np.column_stack([s, np.zeros_like(s), np.zeros_like(s)])
# and the distance travelled from successive marker coordinates
d = np.r_[0, np.cumsum(np.linalg.norm(np.diff(truth[:, :2], axis=0), axis=1))]

NHC remains valid on any path a wheeled platform follows. The synthetic generator only makes out-and-back runs; for an L-shape, add a "turn" entry of 90° to the plan in synth.track_run.

R4 Handheld or drone-style run (no wheels)

NHC assumes a vehicle that cannot slide sideways. A hand or a multirotor can, so turn it off. Stops still work: the operator holds the box still on a stand at each marker (a drone lands or is held).

echo '{"use_nhc": false}' > nav_config_hand.json
python scripts/run_filter.py runs/hand_1 --calib calib.yaml --noise noise.yaml --config "$(cat nav_config_hand.json)"
python tools/gen_calib_header.py calib.yaml --noise noise.yaml --config '{"use_nhc": 0}'

Expect a larger error between stops than on the trolley, since cross-track velocity is now unconstrained while moving. Report both platforms; the difference is the measured value of NHC.

R5 Holybro H-Flow: optical flow as body velocity

The H-Flow speaks DroneCAN, not UART. Read it with a CAN adapter on the laptop (or a CAN transceiver on the ESP32 later) and log flow and range with their own timestamps; convert offline to aux_vel.csv. The flow sensor measures angular rate of the image; subtract the body rotation and scale by height:

\[ v_x = h\,(\omega_{\text{flow},x} - \omega_{y}),\qquad v_y = h\,(\omega_{\text{flow},y} + \omega_{x}) \]

assumed The signs depend on how the board is mounted and on the message convention; fix them empirically: push forward and confirm \(v_x > 0\), rotate in place and confirm \(v \approx 0\).

# scripts/flow_to_aux.py (sketch, ~30 lines)
flow = np.loadtxt("flow_raw.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1)   # t_us, fx, fy [rad/s], range_m, quality
imu = io.load(run).imu
wx = np.interp(flow[:, 0], imu.t_us, imu.gyro[:, 0]); wy = np.interp(flow[:, 0], imu.t_us, imu.gyro[:, 1])
h = flow[:, 3]
ok = (flow[:, 4] > 100) & (h > 0.08)                         # quality and minimum range (80 mm)
vx = h * (flow[:, 1] - wy); vy = h * (flow[:, 2] + wx)
sigma = 0.02 + 0.05 * np.abs(np.c_[vx, vy]).max(axis=1)       # grows with speed; tune with NIS
rows = np.c_[flow[ok, 0], vx[ok], vy[ok], np.full(ok.sum(), np.nan), sigma[ok]]
np.savetxt(f"{run}/aux_vel.csv", rows, delimiter=",", header="t_us,vx,vy,vz,sigma", comments="")

Then run_filter.py uses it automatically. Clock offset between CAN log and IMU: align on a sharp push at the start (cross-correlate flow speed with integrated acceleration). Lever arm between flow sensor and IMU matters only while rotating; mount them within a few centimetres. Rehearse first: make_synth.py track data/s_flow --flow. On synthetic data flow alone does not fix heading drift (it measures velocity in the body frame, which says nothing about yaw), as Chapter 13 predicts.

R6 Wheel encoders (N20 motors)

Forward speed from ticks; lateral and vertical are zero by construction, so a wheel row replaces NHC:

# per encoder sample: v = (dticks_left + dticks_right) / 2 * (2*pi*r_wheel / CPR) / dt
rows.append([t_us, v, 0.0, 0.0, 0.03])                 # aux_vel.csv: vx, vy=0, vz=0, sigma
run_filter.py runs/amr_1 --config '{"use_nhc": false}'

Calibrate r_wheel by driving the 25 m tape and counting ticks; slip appears as NIS spikes. Encoder timestamps must be on the IMU clock: count ticks on the same ESP32 and send them in a new frame type (add to protocol.h and protocol.py).

R7 Position fixes: AprilTags at the fixtures, or UWB

A camera (the D435i colour stream is enough) looking down at a tag taped at a known position gives the IMU position whenever the tag is in view. Write aux_pos.csv with the IMU position, not the camera position:

The tag pose gives the camera position \(\mathbf{p}_C\) in the world; with \(\mathbf{l}_B\) the camera offset from the IMU in the body frame,

\[ \mathbf{p}_{\text{IMU}} = \mathbf{p}_C - \mathbf{R}_{WB}\,\mathbf{l}_B . \]

Subtracting it offline with the estimated attitude works; the cleaner route is to pass the lever arm to the filter, which already supports it: ESKF.position(p_meas, sigma, rows, lever_b=l_B) uses \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{R}\mathbf{l}_B\) and the matching attitude Jacobian. To use it from the runner, change one line in runner.run:

ok, e = f.position(r[1:4][list(axes)], r[4], rows=axes, lever_b=np.array(cfg_lever), gate=...)

Position fixes are ungated by default (gate_aux_pos=false): after a long dark stretch the covariance under-reports the true drift, and a χ² gate would reject the very fix that corrects it. On synthetic data with fixes only at 0 m and 25 m, return-to-start error dropped from 0.89 m to 0.01 m. This is the Tier 3 path (Chapter 13); report it separately from the pure-inertial result.

R8 Phone IMU and magnetometer (Android or iPhone)

Use any logging app that exports gyroscope, accelerometer and magnetometer with timestamps (Sensor Logger and phyphox both work on Android and iOS). Record at the highest rate the app allows; check uncalibrated gyro is selected if the app offers it, so the phone's own bias removal does not fight your filter.

# Android, separate files, time in ns, columns x,y,z
python scripts/convert_phone.py --gyro Gyroscope.csv --acc Accelerometer.csv --mag Magnetometer.csv \
       --time-col time --time-unit ns --out runs/phone_track_1
# iPhone merged export, acceleration in g with the opposite sign
python scripts/convert_phone.py --merged log.csv --time-col loggingTime --time-unit s \
       --gyro-cols gyroX,gyroY,gyroZ --acc-cols accX,accY,accZ --mag-cols magX,magY,magZ \
       --acc-scale -9.80665 --out runs/iphone_track_1
python scripts/check_acq.py runs/phone_track_1 --rate 100

The converter prints \(|\mathbf{a}|\) over the first two seconds: it must be near 9.8. Near 1 means the app writes g (set --acc-scale 9.80665); a negative z when lying screen-up means the sign is flipped (-9.80665). Column names differ between apps and versions; open the CSV and copy them.

Magnetometer: tumble the phone for 60 s away from steel, then fit and survey:

python scripts/run_magcal.py runs/phone_tumble --out magcal.yaml --survey runs/phone_walk
python scripts/run_filter.py runs/phone_track_1 --calib calib_phone.yaml --config '{"use_mag": true, "use_nhc": false}' --magcal magcal.yaml

The runner takes yaw zero from the field direction at alignment, so no declination or true north is needed, and it rejects magnetometer samples whose field magnitude or dip departs from the alignment values by more than 5 % or 3°. On synthetic data with an unmodelled gyro scale error, magnetometer heading cut the maximum error from 0.89 m to 0.22 m; a 5 m disturbed stretch was rejected and the error stayed at 0.23 m. Indoors the survey decides: if \(|\mathbf{m}|\) varies by more than about 10 % along the line, the gate will reject most samples and the magnetometer adds little.

The phone gets the same multi-position calibration as the MPU6050 (lay it on its six faces and many tilts in a box). Its noise is typically lower, its sample timing is worse; run check_acq.py and report both.

R10 Move a host-only aid onto the ESP32

The C update functions exist; what is missing is the input stream and the scheduling. For the magnetometer heading, for example:

  1. Add fields to nav_config_t in nav.h (use_mag, mag_sigma, mag_norm_tol, mag_dip_tol), defaults in nav_default_config, and the same names to C_FIELDS in gen_calib_header.py.
  2. Read the sensor in acq_task and pass it to a new nav_mag(nav_t*, int64_t t_us, const float m[3]) that computes the levelled field, gates it, and calls eskf_heading, exactly as MagHeading.heading in Python.
  3. Add a frame type for the raw magnetometer so the logger records it into mag.csv, and extend host_replay.c to read mag.csv.
  4. Extend test_c_vs_python.py with a run that has mag.csv and use_mag; it must agree to 1 cm.

The MPU6050 has no magnetometer; this path needs a separate magnetometer module on the I²C bus or the phone branch.

Attitude estimation

Attitude is the gateway to position: Chapter 02 showed that 0.1° of tilt error costs metres within 30 s. This chapter builds attitude estimators in order of complexity. The final system estimates attitude inside the ESKF (Chapter 12); Mahony remains as the embedded baseline and the fixed-point bonus route.

1. Tilt from the accelerometer

Intuition. At rest the accelerometer measures the reaction to gravity, which points up. Its direction in the body frame gives roll and pitch.

Derivation Roll and pitch from specific force
  1. Use ZYX Euler angles: \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_z(\psi)\mathbf{R}_y(\theta)\mathbf{R}_x(\phi)\). At rest, \(\mathbf{f}^b = \mathbf{R}^\top[0,0,g]^\top\).
  2. Yaw drops out because \(\mathbf{R}_z(\psi)^\top[0,0,g]^\top = [0,0,g]^\top\). Multiplying out the remaining two rotations: \[ \mathbf{f}^b = g\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \sin\phi\cos\theta\\ \cos\phi\cos\theta\end{bmatrix} \]
  3. Solve: \[ \phi = \operatorname{atan2}(f_y, f_z), \qquad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(-f_x, \sqrt{f_y^2 + f_z^2}\right) \]
  4. Yaw does not appear anywhere, which is the formal statement that gravity cannot observe heading.

Failure mode. When the platform accelerates, \(\mathbf{f}^b = \mathbf{R}^\top(\mathbf{a}^w - \mathbf{g}^w)\) contains \(\mathbf{a}^w\) too, and the formula reports a false tilt of about \(a_h/g\) radians. Pushing the trolley at \(0.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) gives \(0.5/9.81 = 0.051\) rad \(= 2.9^\circ\) of false tilt. Any filter that trusts the accelerometer during acceleration inherits this error.

2. Gyro integration

Intuition. The gyro measures rotation rate, so integrating it tracks attitude through any motion, but every bias integrates into a growing error.

Derivation Quaternion propagation with the exponential map
  1. Kinematics with the body-frame rate: \(\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{q}\otimes[0, \boldsymbol\omega]^\top\).
  2. If \(\boldsymbol\omega\) is constant over \(\Delta t\), the exact solution is \(\mathbf{q}_{k+1} = \mathbf{q}_k\otimes\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol\omega\Delta t)\), where for a rotation vector \(\boldsymbol\phi\): \[ \mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol\phi) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos(\|\boldsymbol\phi\|/2)\\ \dfrac{\boldsymbol\phi}{\|\boldsymbol\phi\|}\sin(\|\boldsymbol\phi\|/2)\end{bmatrix} \]
  3. For \(\|\boldsymbol\phi\| < 10^{-6}\) use \(\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol\phi) \approx [1, \boldsymbol\phi/2]^\top\), then normalise, to avoid division by zero.
  4. Normalise \(\mathbf{q}\) after every step. Rounding errors otherwise make it drift off the unit sphere, and \(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q})\) stops being a rotation.
  5. Numbers: after calibration, a residual of \(0.02^\circ/\mathrm{s}\) integrates to \(1.2^\circ\) of error per minute.

3. Complementary filter

Intuition. The gyro is trustworthy over short times, the accelerometer over long times. Blend them with complementary filters: high-pass the gyro angle, low-pass the accelerometer angle.

Derivation From transfer functions to the discrete update
  1. For one tilt axis, let \(\theta_\omega(s) = \omega(s)/s\) (gyro angle) and \(\theta_a(s)\) (accelerometer angle). Combine \[ \hat\theta(s) = \frac{\tau s}{\tau s + 1}\,\theta_\omega(s) + \frac{1}{\tau s + 1}\,\theta_a(s) \] The two weights sum to 1, so a true angle passes through unchanged; that is what "complementary" means.
  2. Gyro bias \(b\) gives \(\theta_\omega = b/s^2\). Through the high-pass it becomes \(\tau b/(s(\tau s + 1))\), whose steady state is \(\tau b\): a bounded offset instead of a ramp. Accelerometer noise and false tilt during acceleration are attenuated above \(1/\tau\).
  3. Multiply through: \(\hat\theta(\tau s + 1) = \tau\,\omega + \theta_a\), i.e. \(\tau\dot{\hat\theta} = \tau\omega + \theta_a - \hat\theta\).
  4. Discretise with backward Euler: \[ \hat\theta_k = \alpha\left(\hat\theta_{k-1} + \omega_k\Delta t\right) + (1-\alpha)\,\theta_{a,k},\qquad \alpha = \frac{\tau}{\tau + \Delta t} \]
  5. Numbers: \(\tau = 1\) s, \(\Delta t = 5\) ms gives \(\alpha = 0.995\). Residual offset from a \(0.02^\circ/\mathrm{s}\) bias: \(\tau b = 0.02^\circ\).
  6. Limitation: per-axis Euler angles; breaks near \(\theta = \pm 90^\circ\) and couples axes incorrectly during combined rotations. Fine for intuition; use Mahony on quaternions instead.

4. Mahony filter

Intuition. The complementary filter generalised to rotations: compare where the attitude estimate says gravity should point with where the accelerometer says it points, and feed the angular difference back into the gyro rate. An integral term learns the gyro bias.

Derivation Mahony nonlinear complementary filter on SO(3)
  1. Predicted gravity direction in the body frame from the estimate: \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})^\top\mathbf{e}_z\), with \(\mathbf{e}_z = [0,0,1]^\top\). Measured direction: \(\bar{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{f}_m/\|\mathbf{f}_m\|\).
  2. Error vector: \(\mathbf{e} = \bar{\mathbf{v}}\times\hat{\mathbf{v}}\). Its magnitude is \(\sin\) of the angle between them; its direction is the axis about which the estimate must rotate to align them.
  3. Corrected rate and bias law: \[ \boldsymbol\omega_c = \boldsymbol\omega_m - \hat{\mathbf{b}}_g + k_P\,\mathbf{e}, \qquad \dot{\hat{\mathbf{b}}}_g = -k_I\,\mathbf{e} \]
  4. Propagate: \(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+1} = \hat{\mathbf{q}}_k\otimes\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol\omega_c\Delta t)\), normalise.
  5. Linearised for small errors and one axis, the attitude error obeys a second-order system with natural frequency \(\sqrt{k_I}\) and damping \(k_P/(2\sqrt{k_I})\); \(k_P\) plays the role of \(1/\tau\). Mahony, Hamel and Pflimlin prove almost-global stability with a Lyapunov function \(V = \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{R}}) + \tfrac{1}{2k_I}\|\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\|^2\).
  6. Typical gains: \(k_P = 1\) to \(2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(k_I = 0.01\) to \(0.05\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\). Tune on the static and push tests below.
  7. Acceleration gate: skip the correction (set \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\)) when \(\left|\,\|\mathbf{f}_m\| - g\,\right| > 0.05g\) or the gyro rate is high. This removes most false-tilt corruption on the trolley.
  8. Cost: one quaternion-to-vector rotation, one cross product, one quaternion product and normalisation: on the order of 100 floating-point operations per step.

5. Madgwick filter

Intuition. Take one gradient-descent step per sample towards the attitude whose predicted gravity matches the accelerometer, and fuse that step with gyro integration.

Derivation Objective, Jacobian and update
  1. With normalised accelerometer \(\bar{\mathbf{a}}\), the objective is \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q})^\top\mathbf{e}_z - \bar{\mathbf{a}}\). Written out for \(\mathbf{q} = [q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]\): \[ \mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix}2(q_xq_z - q_wq_y) - \bar a_x\\ 2(q_wq_x + q_yq_z) - \bar a_y\\ 1 - 2q_x^2 - 2q_y^2 - \bar a_z\end{bmatrix} \]
  2. Jacobian with respect to \((q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z)\): \[ \mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix}-2q_y & 2q_z & -2q_w & 2q_x\\ 2q_x & 2q_w & 2q_z & 2q_y\\ 0 & -4q_x & -4q_y & 0\end{bmatrix} \]
  3. Gradient of \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf{f}\|^2\): \(\nabla = \mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{f}\).
  4. Update: \[ \dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{q}\otimes[0, \boldsymbol\omega_m]^\top - \beta\,\frac{\nabla}{\|\nabla\|} \] integrate over \(\Delta t\), normalise.
  5. Madgwick chooses \(\beta = \sqrt{3/4}\,\bar\omega_\beta\), with \(\bar\omega_\beta\) the expected gyro error magnitude in rad/s.
  6. Differences from Mahony: one gain, no bias estimate in the basic form, a fixed correction rate set by \(\beta\) rather than proportional to the error. Accuracy is similar in published comparisons; Mahony is chosen here because its bias estimate and its simple fixed-point form suit our needs.

6. EKF orientation

An EKF with state \(\{\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{b}_g\}\), gyro propagation and the accelerometer as a measurement of \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{g}\) is the attitude-only subset of the ESKF in Chapter 12: keep only the \(\delta\boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\delta\mathbf{b}_g\) blocks. Its advantage over Mahony is a covariance, which tells you how uncertain the attitude is and weights the accelerometer accordingly. We do not build it separately.

Heading

Gravity fixes roll and pitch only. Heading needs an external reference. A magnetometer is the usual one, but indoors steel structures, rebar and electronics distort the field by tens of degrees, so magnetometer heading needs disturbance checks on field magnitude and dip angle. The MPU6050 has no magnetometer anyway. Our heading comes from:

  1. Initial yaw defined by the start fixture (yaw = 0 along the track).
  2. Gyro integration, with the z-gyro bias re-estimated at every stop (ZARU).
  3. Optionally, the straight-line constraint, which makes cross-track position, and through it heading, observable while moving (Chapter 13).

Kalman filters from first principles

The Kalman filter keeps a Gaussian belief about the state, moves it forward with a model, and corrects it with each measurement in proportion to how much it trusts each side.

Linear model

\[ \mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{w}_k,\quad \mathbf{w}_k\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}) \qquad \mathbf{z}_k = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{v}_k,\quad \mathbf{v}_k\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R}) \]
SymbolSizeMeaning
\(\mathbf{x}\)\(n\times1\)State
\(\mathbf{F}\)\(n\times n\)State transition over one step
\(\mathbf{Q}\)\(n\times n\)Process noise covariance: how much the model can be wrong per step
\(\mathbf{z}\)\(m\times1\)Measurement
\(\mathbf{H}\)\(m\times n\)Measurement matrix: which combination of states is measured
\(\mathbf{R}\)\(m\times m\)Measurement noise covariance
\(\mathbf{P}\)\(n\times n\)Covariance of the estimation error
Derivation Prediction
  1. Mean: \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = \mathbb E[\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{w}] = \mathbf{F}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}\), since \(\mathbf{w}\) has zero mean.
  2. Error: \(\mathbf{x}_k - \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}) + \mathbf{w}\).
  3. Covariance, using independence of the previous error and \(\mathbf{w}\): \[ \mathbf{P}^- = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^\top + \mathbf{Q} \]
Derivation Update by Gaussian conditioning
  1. Before the measurement, \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) are jointly Gaussian: \[ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ \mathbf{z}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\left(\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-\\ \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}^- & \mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top\\ \mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}^- & \mathbf{S}\end{bmatrix}\right),\qquad \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top + \mathbf{R} \]
  2. For jointly Gaussian \((\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})\), \(\mathbb E[\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b}] = \boldsymbol\mu_a + \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{b} - \boldsymbol\mu_b)\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b}] = \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} - \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba}\).
  3. Define innovation \(\boldsymbol\nu = \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-\) (size \(m\)) and gain \(\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\) (size \(n\times m\)).
  4. Result: \[ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- + \mathbf{K}\boldsymbol\nu,\qquad \mathbf{P} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}^- \]
  5. Interpretation: \(\mathbf{S}\) is the predicted spread of the innovation. \(\mathbf{K}\) is large where the prior is uncertain relative to the measurement, and only states correlated with the measured ones (through \(\mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top\)) get corrected. This is how a velocity measurement fixes accelerometer bias: by correlation built up during prediction.
Derivation Joseph form and why to use it
  1. Write the posterior error for any gain \(\mathbf{K}\): \(\mathbf{e}^+ = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{e}^- - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}\).
  2. Its covariance: \[ \mathbf{P} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}^-(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})^\top + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}^\top \]
  3. This is valid for any \(\mathbf{K}\), symmetric by construction and positive semidefinite as a sum of such terms. The short form \((\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}^-\) is equal only for the exact optimal gain; with float32 rounding it loses symmetry and can produce negative variances. Use Joseph on the ESP32, then symmetrise: \(\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\tfrac12(\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{P}^\top)\).
Worked example One-axis velocity with a ZUPT
  1. State: along-track velocity error. \(F = 1\), \(Q = N_a^2\Delta t\) with \(N_a = 3.92\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(\Delta t = 5\) ms.
  2. After a 10 s move from \(P = 0\): \(P^- = N_a^2 \times 10 = 1.54\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), so \(\sigma_v = 0.0124\) m/s.
  3. One ZUPT sample, \(z = 0\), \(H = 1\), \(R = (0.01\ \mathrm{m/s})^2 = 10^{-4}\): \(S = 2.54\times10^{-4}\), \(K = 0.606\).
  4. Posterior: \(P = (1 - 0.606)\times1.54\times10^{-4} = 6.07\times10^{-5}\), so \(\sigma_v\) falls to \(0.0078\) m/s after one sample. Over a 3 s stop at 200 Hz, 600 such updates drive it towards zero.
  5. Implementation link: this is exactly the \(\delta\mathbf{v}\) block of the ESKF's ZUPT, one axis at a time. Use it as a unit test: the Python ESKF with all other states frozen must reproduce these numbers.

Consistency and gating

If the filter's model is right, the normalised innovation squared \(\epsilon_\nu = \boldsymbol\nu^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\boldsymbol\nu\) follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m\) degrees of freedom. Its average over a run should be close to \(m\). Much larger means \(\mathbf{Q}\) or \(\mathbf{R}\) is too small (overconfident); much smaller means too large. With synthetic truth, the normalised estimation error squared \(\epsilon_x = \mathbf{e}^\top\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\) should average \(n\).

Gating: reject a measurement when \(\epsilon_\nu\) exceeds the 99% \(\chi^2\) quantile: 6.63 for \(m = 1\), 9.21 for \(m = 2\), 11.34 for \(m = 3\). Log every rejection; a stream of rejections is a symptom, not a fix.

Extended Kalman filter

Derivation Linearisation
  1. Nonlinear model: \(\mathbf{x}_k = f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_k) + \mathbf{w}\), \(\mathbf{z} = h(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{v}\).
  2. Propagate the mean through the full nonlinear \(f\): \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = f(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{u})\).
  3. First-order Taylor expansion around the estimate: \(f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}})\) with \(\mathbf{F} = \partial f/\partial\mathbf{x}\big|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}\). Then \(\mathbf{P}^- = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^\top + \mathbf{Q}\).
  4. Likewise \(\mathbf{H} = \partial h/\partial\mathbf{x}\big|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-}\), innovation \(\boldsymbol\nu = \mathbf{z} - h(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-)\), and the linear update equations.
  5. Failure cases: large errors where the linearisation is poor; Jacobians evaluated at a wrong estimate produce overconfidence; and constrained states. A unit quaternion has four numbers but three degrees of freedom, so a 4×4 covariance on it is singular in the normal direction and updates push \(\mathbf{q}\) off the unit sphere. The error-state formulation removes this problem.
Derivation Continuous to discrete noise
  1. Continuous error model: \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\,\delta\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{n}\), with white \(\mathbf{n}\) of spectral density \(\mathbf{Q}_c\).
  2. Exact transition: \(\mathbf{F} = e^{\mathbf{A}\Delta t} \approx \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}\Delta t\) for small \(\Delta t\).
  3. Exact discrete noise: \(\mathbf{Q}_d = \int_0^{\Delta t} e^{\mathbf{A}s}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q}_c\mathbf{L}^\top e^{\mathbf{A}^\top s}ds \approx \mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q}_c\mathbf{L}^\top\Delta t\) to first order.
  4. At 200 Hz the first-order forms are accurate; the error terms are of order \(\|\mathbf{A}\|\Delta t\), and the largest entries of \(\mathbf{A}\) (gravity coupling, about 10) times 5 ms is 0.05. Van Loan's method gives the exact integral if a check is wanted.

Error-state Kalman filter

The ESKF propagates a nominal state with the full nonlinear strapdown equations and runs a Kalman filter only on the small error between the nominal and the true state. The error is small, so its dynamics are nearly linear and the attitude error needs only three numbers.

Conventions (fixed for the code)

  • Hamilton quaternions, scalar first, \(\mathbf{q}\) maps body to world.
  • Local (right) perturbation: \(\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{q}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q}\), \(\delta\mathbf{q}\approx[1, \tfrac12\delta\boldsymbol\theta]^\top\), so \(\mathbf{R}_t = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I} + [\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times)\). \(\delta\boldsymbol\theta\) is expressed in the body frame. Subscript \(t\) means true.
  • Measurements after calibration: \(\mathbf{f}_m = \mathbf{f}_t + \mathbf{b}_{a,t} + \mathbf{n}_a\), \(\boldsymbol\omega_m = \boldsymbol\omega_t + \mathbf{b}_{g,t} + \mathbf{n}_g\). The biases here are the residuals left after calibration.
  • \([\mathbf{a}]_\times\) is the skew matrix with \([\mathbf{a}]_\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\); note \([\mathbf{a}]_\times\mathbf{b} = -[\mathbf{b}]_\times\mathbf{a}\).

Why three numbers for attitude: a unit quaternion has one constraint, so rotations have three degrees of freedom. A small rotation is fully described by the rotation vector \(\delta\boldsymbol\theta\), which has no constraint, so its 3×3 covariance block is regular.

Nominal propagation

\[ \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{f}_m - \mathbf{b}_a) + \mathbf{g}^w,\quad \mathbf{p}\leftarrow\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{v}\Delta t + \tfrac12\mathbf{a}\Delta t^2,\quad \mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{a}\Delta t,\quad \mathbf{q}\leftarrow\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathrm{Exp}\big((\boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g)\Delta t\big) \]

Biases are constant in the nominal model. Normalise \(\mathbf{q}\). Order matters: use the attitude at the start of the step for \(\mathbf{a}\), then update \(\mathbf{q}\) (or use the midpoint attitude for slightly better accuracy; be consistent in Python and C).

Derivation Velocity error dynamics
  1. True dynamics: \(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{R}_t(\mathbf{f}_m - \mathbf{b}_{a,t} - \mathbf{n}_a) + \mathbf{g}^w\).
  2. Substitute \(\mathbf{R}_t = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I} + [\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times)\) and \(\mathbf{b}_{a,t} = \mathbf{b}_a + \delta\mathbf{b}_a\), and write \(\hat{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{f}_m - \mathbf{b}_a\): \[ \dot{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I} + [\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times)(\hat{\mathbf{f}} - \delta\mathbf{b}_a - \mathbf{n}_a) + \mathbf{g}^w \]
  3. Expand and drop products of error terms (second order): \[ \dot{\mathbf{v}}_t \approx \mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}} + \mathbf{R}[\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times\hat{\mathbf{f}} - \mathbf{R}\,\delta\mathbf{b}_a - \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{n}_a + \mathbf{g}^w \]
  4. Subtract the nominal \(\dot{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}} + \mathbf{g}^w\) and use \([\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times\hat{\mathbf{f}} = -[\hat{\mathbf{f}}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta\): \[ \delta\dot{\mathbf{v}} = -\mathbf{R}[\hat{\mathbf{f}}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{R}\,\delta\mathbf{b}_a - \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{n}_a \]
  5. Check against Chapter 02: level and at rest, \(\hat{\mathbf{f}} = [0,0,g]^\top\), \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\). Then \(-[\hat{\mathbf{f}}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta = [g\,\delta\theta_y,\ -g\,\delta\theta_x,\ 0]^\top\): a pitch error \(\delta\theta_y\) produces an \(x\) acceleration error \(g\,\delta\theta_y\), exactly the tilt term derived earlier.
Derivation Attitude error dynamics
  1. Write \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} = \boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g\) (nominal) and \(\boldsymbol\omega_t = \boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g - \delta\mathbf{b}_g - \mathbf{n}_g\) (true). Treat rates as pure quaternions \([0, \boldsymbol\omega]\).
  2. Differentiate \(\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{q}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q}\) two ways: \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t = \tfrac12\mathbf{q}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q}\otimes\boldsymbol\omega_t\) and \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t = \dot{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q} + \mathbf{q}\otimes\dot{\delta\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac12\mathbf{q}\otimes\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q} + \mathbf{q}\otimes\dot{\delta\mathbf{q}}\).
  3. Equate and cancel \(\mathbf{q}\otimes\): \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac12\left(\delta\mathbf{q}\otimes\boldsymbol\omega_t - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\otimes\delta\mathbf{q}\right)\).
  4. With \(\delta\mathbf{q} = [1, \tfrac12\delta\boldsymbol\theta]\), the product rule for quaternions gives vector parts \(\boldsymbol\omega_t + \tfrac12\delta\boldsymbol\theta\times\boldsymbol\omega_t\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} + \tfrac12\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta\).
  5. Subtract, keep first order (\(\boldsymbol\omega_t\approx\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) inside the cross product): vector part of \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{q}}\) is \(\tfrac12\left[(\boldsymbol\omega_t - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta\right]\). It equals \(\tfrac12\dot{\delta\boldsymbol\theta}\), so \[ \dot{\delta\boldsymbol\theta} = -[\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta - \delta\mathbf{b}_g - \mathbf{n}_g \]

Position and biases: \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{p}} = \delta\mathbf{v}\), \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{b}}_a = \mathbf{n}_{ba}\), \(\dot{\delta\mathbf{b}}_g = \mathbf{n}_{bg}\) (random walks).

Discrete transition and noise

\[ \mathbf{F} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{I}\Delta t & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I} & -\mathbf{R}[\hat{\mathbf{f}}]_\times\Delta t & -\mathbf{R}\Delta t & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}\{\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\Delta t\}^\top & \mathbf{0} & -\mathbf{I}\Delta t\\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{Q}_d = \mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{0},\ N_a^2\Delta t\,\mathbf{I},\ N_g^2\Delta t\,\mathbf{I},\ K_a^2\Delta t\,\mathbf{I},\ K_g^2\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}\right) \]

\(\mathbf{R}\{\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\Delta t\}\) is the rotation matrix of \(\mathrm{Exp}(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\Delta t)\); its transpose is the exact solution of the \(-[\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}]_\times\) block. The velocity noise block is \(\mathbf{R}(N_a^2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{R}^\top\Delta t = N_a^2\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}\) because the noise is isotropic. \(N_a, N_g\) come from Allan deviation (Chapter 08), \(K_a, K_g\) from the bias random walk or the bias-instability approximation.

Measurement update, injection and reset

  1. For a measurement \(\mathbf{z}\) with model \(h(\mathbf{x}_t)\): innovation \(\boldsymbol\nu = \mathbf{z} - h(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\), Jacobian \(\mathbf{H} = \partial h/\partial\delta\mathbf{x}\) at the nominal state.
  2. \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^\top + \mathbf{R}_z\); gate on \(\boldsymbol\nu^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\boldsymbol\nu\); \(\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\); \(\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{K}\boldsymbol\nu\); Joseph-form \(\mathbf{P}\).
  3. Inject: \(\mathbf{p}\mathrel{+}=\delta\hat{\mathbf{p}}\), \(\mathbf{v}\mathrel{+}=\delta\hat{\mathbf{v}}\), \(\mathbf{q}\leftarrow\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathrm{Exp}(\delta\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})\), \(\mathbf{b}_a\mathrel{+}=\delta\hat{\mathbf{b}}_a\), \(\mathbf{b}_g\mathrel{+}=\delta\hat{\mathbf{b}}_g\); normalise \(\mathbf{q}\).
  4. Reset: the error is now redefined around the new nominal attitude, so \(\mathbf{P}\leftarrow\mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{G}^\top\) with \(\mathbf{G} = \mathrm{blkdiag}(\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{I}, \mathbf{I} - [\tfrac12\delta\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}]_\times, \mathbf{I}, \mathbf{I})\). For small corrections \(\mathbf{G}\approx\mathbf{I}\); include it anyway, it costs little.
Jacobians ZUPT and ZARU

ZUPT. When stationary, \(\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{0}\). Model \(h = \mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{v} + \delta\mathbf{v}\), measurement \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\boldsymbol\nu = -\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{H} = [\mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{I}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}]\), \(\mathbf{R}_z = \sigma_{\mathrm{zupt}}^2\mathbf{I}\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zupt}}\approx 0.01\) m/s (trolley vibration while "still").

ZARU. When stationary, \(\boldsymbol\omega_t = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\boldsymbol\omega_m = \mathbf{b}_{g,t} + \mathbf{n}_g\). Model \(h = \mathbf{b}_g + \delta\mathbf{b}_g\), measurement \(\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol\omega_m\), \(\boldsymbol\nu = \boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g\), \(\mathbf{H} = [\mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{I}]\), \(\mathbf{R}_z = \sigma_{\mathrm{zaru}}^2\mathbf{I}\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zaru}}\) a few times the gyro per-sample noise \(N_g/\sqrt{\Delta t}\).

Gravity update at rest (optional). \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{f}_m\), \(h = \mathbf{R}_t^\top[0,0,g]^\top + \mathbf{b}_{a,t}\). With \(\mathbf{R}_t^\top = (\mathbf{I} - [\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times)\mathbf{R}^\top\) and \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{R}^\top[0,0,g]^\top\): \(h\approx\mathbf{u} + [\mathbf{u}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta + \mathbf{b}_a + \delta\mathbf{b}_a\), so \(\mathbf{H} = [\mathbf{0}\ \ \mathbf{0}\ \ [\mathbf{u}]_\times\ \ \mathbf{I}\ \ \mathbf{0}]\). It shows the tilt and horizontal accel bias ambiguity directly: both columns predict the same horizontal residual when level.

import numpy as np
G = 9.80665
def skew(a): return np.array([[0,-a[2],a[1]],[a[2],0,-a[0]],[-a[1],a[0],0]])
def qmul(p, q):
    w1,x1,y1,z1 = p; w2,x2,y2,z2 = q
    return np.array([w1*w2-x1*x2-y1*y2-z1*z2, w1*x2+x1*w2+y1*z2-z1*y2,
                     w1*y2-x1*z2+y1*w2+z1*x2, w1*z2+x1*y2-y1*x2+z1*w2])
def qexp(phi):
    a = np.linalg.norm(phi)
    if a < 1e-9: q = np.r_[1.0, 0.5*phi]
    else:        q = np.r_[np.cos(a/2), np.sin(a/2)*phi/a]
    return q/np.linalg.norm(q)
def q2R(q):
    w,x,y,z = q
    return np.array([[1-2*(y*y+z*z), 2*(x*y-w*z),   2*(x*z+w*y)],
                     [2*(x*y+w*z),   1-2*(x*x+z*z), 2*(y*z-w*x)],
                     [2*(x*z-w*y),   2*(y*z+w*x),   1-2*(x*x+y*y)]])

class ESKF:
    def __init__(s, q0, bg0, P0, Na, Ng, Kba, Kbg):
        s.p = np.zeros(3); s.v = np.zeros(3); s.q = q0; s.ba = np.zeros(3); s.bg = bg0
        s.P = P0; s.Na, s.Ng, s.Kba, s.Kbg = Na, Ng, Kba, Kbg
        s.gw = np.array([0, 0, -G])

    def predict(s, f_m, w_m, dt):
        R = q2R(s.q); f = f_m - s.ba; w = w_m - s.bg
        a = R @ f + s.gw
        s.p += s.v*dt + 0.5*a*dt*dt
        s.v += a*dt
        dq = qexp(w*dt); s.q = qmul(s.q, dq); s.q /= np.linalg.norm(s.q)
        F = np.eye(15)
        F[0:3, 3:6]   = np.eye(3)*dt
        F[3:6, 6:9]   = -R @ skew(f) * dt
        F[3:6, 9:12]  = -R*dt
        F[6:9, 6:9]   = q2R(dq).T
        F[6:9, 12:15] = -np.eye(3)*dt
        Qd = np.diag(np.r_[np.zeros(3), np.full(3, s.Na**2*dt), np.full(3, s.Ng**2*dt),
                           np.full(3, s.Kba**2*dt), np.full(3, s.Kbg**2*dt)])
        s.P = F @ s.P @ F.T + Qd

    def update(s, nu, H, Rz, gate=None):
        S = H @ s.P @ H.T + Rz
        nis = float(nu @ np.linalg.solve(S, nu))
        if gate is not None and nis > gate: return False, nis
        K = s.P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
        dx = K @ nu
        IKH = np.eye(15) - K @ H
        s.P = IKH @ s.P @ IKH.T + K @ Rz @ K.T
        s.P = 0.5*(s.P + s.P.T)
        s.inject(dx); return True, nis

    def inject(s, dx):
        s.p += dx[0:3]; s.v += dx[3:6]
        s.q = qmul(s.q, qexp(dx[6:9])); s.q /= np.linalg.norm(s.q)
        s.ba += dx[9:12]; s.bg += dx[12:15]
        Gm = np.eye(15); Gm[6:9, 6:9] -= skew(0.5*dx[6:9])
        s.P = Gm @ s.P @ Gm.T

    def zupt(s, sigma=0.01):
        H = np.zeros((3, 15)); H[:, 3:6] = np.eye(3)
        return s.update(-s.v, H, sigma**2*np.eye(3), gate=11.34)

    def zaru(s, w_m, sigma):
        H = np.zeros((3, 15)); H[:, 12:15] = np.eye(3)
        return s.update(w_m - s.bg, H, sigma**2*np.eye(3), gate=11.34)

Aiding and observability

Each aid constrains specific parts of the error state. Knowing which parts, and which stay unconstrained, tells you what an aid can and cannot fix before you build it.

Two kinds of measurement appear here. Physical measurements come from a sensor (optical flow, wheel encoder, tag camera, UWB radio). Pseudo-measurements come from known facts about the motion (stationary, no sideslip, on the line). Pseudo-measurements are valid only while the fact is true, so each needs a detector or a validity condition, and its noise \(\mathbf{R}_z\) must cover how imperfectly the fact holds.

Decision tree

Guaranteed aids

Aid ZUPT and ZARU with the SHOE detector
  1. Principle. During a waypoint stop or hover hold, true velocity and angular rate are zero.
  2. Measurement and Jacobian. Chapter 12: \(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{zupt}} = [\mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{I}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}]\), \(\boldsymbol\nu = -\mathbf{v}\); \(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{zaru}} = [\mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{I}]\), \(\boldsymbol\nu = \boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g\).
  3. Detector (Skog et al., SHOE, window \(W\) samples): \[ T_n = \frac{1}{W}\sum_{k=n}^{n+W-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_a^2}\left\|\mathbf{f}_k - g\frac{\bar{\mathbf{f}}_n}{\|\bar{\mathbf{f}}_n\|}\right\|^2 + \frac{1}{\sigma_\omega^2}\|\boldsymbol\omega_k\|^2\right) < \gamma \] with \(\bar{\mathbf{f}}_n\) the window mean. Typical \(W\) = 0.1 to 0.2 s. Choose \(\gamma\) from the track runs: mark true stop intervals using the marker button and sweep \(\gamma\) for zero false positives during motion.
  4. Noise. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zupt}}\approx 0.01\) m/s; increase if the trolley rocks when it stops.
  5. Validity and gating. A false ZUPT during slow motion is far more harmful than a missed one, because it injects a wrong velocity with high confidence. Require \(T_n < \gamma\) for a minimum duration (for example 0.3 s) before applying; gate each update on NIS.
  6. Failure cases. Very slow creeping (detected as stopped), vibration from a running motor or a hand (missed stop), the trolley rolling slightly after you let go.
Aid Non-holonomic constraint (trolley mode)
  1. Principle. A wheeled vehicle on a flat floor neither slides sideways nor leaves the floor: body-frame lateral and vertical velocity are zero.
  2. Measurement. \(\mathbf{z} = [0, 0]^\top\), \(h = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}_t^\top\mathbf{v}_t\) with \(\mathbf{S} = [\mathbf{e}_y, \mathbf{e}_z]^\top\).
  3. Jacobian. \(\mathbf{R}_t^\top = (\mathbf{I} - [\delta\boldsymbol\theta]_\times)\mathbf{R}^\top\), so \(h\approx\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^\top\delta\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{S}[\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{v}]_\times\delta\boldsymbol\theta\): \[ \mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^\top & \mathbf{S}[\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{v}]_\times & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix} \]
  4. Noise. \(\sigma\approx 0.02\) to 0.05 m/s; caster wobble and the IMU not being at the rotation centre add apparent lateral velocity \(\omega_z\times\) lever arm. Either compensate the lever arm or inflate \(\sigma\) with \(|\omega_z|\).
  5. Validity. Only on the trolley. Disabled in handheld mode and during the 180° turn (detect \(|\omega_z|\) above a threshold).
  6. Observability. While moving, a heading error makes the estimated velocity point sideways in the body frame, which the NHC sees. So NHC observes heading relative to the direction of motion, not absolute heading.
Aid Straight-line map constraint
  1. Principle. The reference point of the box stays on the taped line \(y^w = 0\), within how well the trolley is steered.
  2. Measurement. \(z = 0\), \(h = p_y + \delta p_y\), \(\nu = -p_y\), \(\mathbf{H} = [\mathbf{e}_y^\top\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}\ \mathbf{0}]\) (one row).
  3. Noise. \(\sigma\) = the actual lateral deviation of the pointer from the tape while pushing, measured once (likely 3 to 5 cm). Apply at a low rate (for example 10 Hz) because the deviation is correlated over time, and applying it at 200 Hz with independent-noise assumptions makes the filter overconfident.
  4. Independence. Uses only the line's shape; marker positions stay out of the filter (Chapter 05).
  5. Observability. Fixes cross-track position, and through its time history, yaw. Along-track position remains unconstrained.
  6. Reporting. Strong prior; judges may consider it near ground truth. Always report the result with it off as well.

Synthetic data and simulation

Synthetic data has exact ground truth, so it finds frame, sign and equation bugs that real data hides. It becomes evidence only when its error model comes from your measured sensor and it contains effects the filter does not model.

From trajectory to ideal IMU signals

Derivation Ideal specific force and angular rate
  1. Define \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) and \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) as smooth functions (twice differentiable for position, once for attitude).
  2. Angular rate in the body frame, from \(\dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}[\boldsymbol\omega^b]_\times\): \[ [\boldsymbol\omega^b]_\times = \mathbf{R}^\top\dot{\mathbf{R}} \] Extract the three components from the skew matrix.
  3. Specific force: \[ \mathbf{f}^b = \mathbf{R}^\top\left(\ddot{\mathbf{p}} - \mathbf{g}^w\right) \] At rest, level: \([0, 0, +g]^\top\).
  4. Our track: straight segments along \(x^w\) with yaw \(\psi = 0\) going out and \(\psi = \pi\) coming back. On a segment of length \(\Delta s\) and duration \(T\), use the minimum-jerk profile \(s(\tau) = s_0 + \Delta s(10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5)\), \(\tau = t/T\), which starts and ends with zero velocity and acceleration. Its peak acceleration is \(5.77\,\Delta s/T^2\) and peak speed \(1.875\,\Delta s/T\): for 5 m in 10 s, 0.29 m/s² and 0.94 m/s.
  5. Turn at 25 m: yaw follows the same minimum-jerk shape from 0 to \(\pi\) while stationary, so \(\boldsymbol\omega^b = [0, 0, \dot\psi]^\top\).
  6. Add realism the filter does not model: small roll and pitch oscillation from floor bumps (a few tenths of a degree, a few hertz), and the lever arm between the box reference point and the IMU.

Applying the measured error model

Model Corrupting ideal signals
  1. Calibration maps raw to true: \(\mathbf{a}^O = \mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a(\mathbf{a}^S + \mathbf{b}_a)\). The generator inverts it: \(\mathbf{a}^S = (\mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a)^{-1}\mathbf{a}^O - \mathbf{b}_a\), likewise for the gyro.
  2. Two uses. (a) Full parameters from calib.yaml produce raw data to test the calibration tool A1: it must recover the parameters it was given. (b) Residual parameters (the fit's uncertainty, from test C3) produce realistic calibrated data to test the filter.
  3. Biases: random walk \(\mathbf{b}_{k+1} = \mathbf{b}_k + K\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\boldsymbol\eta_k\), plus a temperature term from test C4.
  4. White noise: per-sample \(\sigma = N/\sqrt{\Delta t}\) from Allan deviation.
  5. Quantisation to the MPU6050 LSB at the chosen range (1/65.5 °/s, 1/8192 g); saturation at full scale.
  6. Timestamp jitter and occasional dropped samples, at the rates measured in test A1.
  7. Every dataset writes its seed and all parameters into config.yaml.
Inverse crime Generating data with exactly the filter's own model and noise values, then reporting the filter's accuracy on it, proves only that the code is self-consistent. Use synthetic data to find bugs and test consistency (NEES), and include unmodelled effects (temperature drift, scale residuals, vibration, lever arm, jitter). Report real-track results as the accuracy evidence.

Generator self-tests

  1. Noise-free signals integrated with the strapdown equations reproduce the trajectory to numerical precision. If not, the generator or the integrator has a frame bug, and C2 catches it before B depends on it.
  2. Static segments read \([0, 0, g]\) and zero rate.
  3. Calibration round trip: generate raw data from known parameters, fit with A1, recover the parameters within their stated uncertainty.

Fixed point (Tier 3 route)

The tier text says "run the full pipeline in fixed-point arithmetic on a microcontroller". Our interpretation assumed: calibration correction, Mahony attitude, strapdown propagation, stop detection, ZUPT velocity reset and ZARU bias update, all in integer arithmetic. A fixed-point 15-state ESKF would need a square-root or UDU covariance form to stay positive definite, and is out of scope. Confirm this reading with the organizers.

Design Choosing formats by resolution and range

A Q\(m.n\) number stores \(x\cdot2^n\) in an integer with \(m\) integer bits. Each format must cover the range and resolve the smallest increment that matters.

QuantityRangeFormatResolution
Quaternion components±1, transiently above 1 before normalisationQ2.30 in int32\(9.3\times10^{-10}\)
Angular rate±8.73 rad/s (±500 °/s)Q5.27 in int32\(7.5\times10^{-9}\) rad/s
Specific force±39.2 m/s² (±4 g)Q7.25 in int32\(3.0\times10^{-8}\) m/s²

  1. The trap: increments, not values. Each step adds \(\mathbf{a}\Delta t\) to velocity. An acceleration error of 1 mg over one 5 ms step is \(4.9\times10^{-5}\) m/s. In Q16.16 velocity (resolution \(1.5\times10^{-5}\)) that is only three LSBs, so smaller errors and slow accelerations are truncated away, and truncation is biased. That is why velocity and position accumulate in int64 Q32.32 even though their values would fit in 32 bits.
  2. Multiply two int32 values into an int64, then shift right with rounding (add half an LSB before the shift).
  3. Saturate instead of wrapping on overflow.
  4. Normalise the quaternion with an integer Newton iteration for \(1/\sqrt{x}\), which converges in two or three iterations near 1.
  5. Validation: run the fixed-point and the float64 pipelines on the same logs in HIL replay and plot the difference over time. The fixed-point result should differ by far less than the estimation error, with no growing trend.

Likely questions

Why should we believe your calibration worked?

Because it was tested on data it was not fitted to: held-out static poses (gravity-norm error before and after) and rotations against a square edge. Parameters were repeatable across three sessions, within their estimated uncertainty, and an independent implementation (IMU-TK) gave matching values.

Why an ESKF and not a UKF or an EKF on the full state?

The attitude error is three-dimensional and small, so its dynamics are close to linear and the UKF's extra cost buys nothing measurable at our error sizes. A full-state EKF on a quaternion has a singular covariance direction; the error state avoids it. The ESKF is also the standard structure in PX4's estimator, so it is well understood.

Isn't ZUPT cheating?

ZUPT uses a fact the platform genuinely has: robots stop at waypoints and drones hold hover. It is on the permitted list. It constrains velocity only, never position, which is why along-track error still grows and why the stop spacing experiment shows that growth.

Isn't the straight-line constraint ground truth?

It uses only the path shape, not marker positions or times, which stay out of the filter. It constrains cross-track position only. We report every result with and without it, so you can judge it separately.

Where does your heading come from without a magnetometer?

The start fixture defines initial heading, the gyro propagates it, and ZARU re-estimates the z-gyro bias at every stop. The residual heading drift is visible as cross-track error in the no-line-constraint results, and its size matches the bias residual we measured.

How did you pick Q and R?

Q from Allan deviation of our own boards, inflated for effects a static test cannot see, and checked by the innovation statistics on training runs: mean NIS inside its 95% band. R for pseudo-measurements from how imperfectly the condition holds, for example the trolley's measured lateral deviation from the tape.

How do you know the filter isn't overconfident?

NEES on synthetic Monte Carlo runs with known truth, and NIS on real runs, both inside their \(\chi^2\) bands. A gated measurement is logged; we report how many were rejected.

Does it really run in real time on the ESP32?

Yes, measured: cycle counts per step against the 5 ms budget, zero missed samples over 30 minutes, and agreement with the float64 Python reference on identical input.

What did simulation contribute?

Bug finding with exact truth, consistency testing and the stop-spacing sensitivity study. Its noise model came from our measured sensor and included effects the filter does not model. No accuracy claim rests on simulation; the scored results are real track runs.

Why does your accuracy differ between runs?

Bias residuals change with temperature and each stop's bias estimate has noise. The spread across runs matches the Monte Carlo spread predicted from the measured noise, which is the evidence that it is noise and not a systematic error.

What would you do with more time?

A velocity sensor (optical flow fits both drones and robots), a better IMU (lower bias instability directly reduces the dominant error term), temperature-compensated calibration, and tightly coupled tag updates for the sub-0.3 m tier.

Final architecture decision

MPU6050 in a rigid box, read through its FIFO by an original ESP32 with interrupt timestamps; multi-position calibration fitted offline and flashed as constants; a 15-state float32 ESKF on the ESP32 with ZUPT, ZARU, NHC and a straight-line constraint; a float64 Python reference and HIL replay on the host. Chosen because it reaches every scoring criterion with guaranteed hardware, and each optional aid can be added as a branch without changing the core.
